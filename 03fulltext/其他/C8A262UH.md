Abstract—Wireless resource allocation is a critical component
in modern communication systems, and deep neural networks
(DNNs) have shown great promise in addressing this challenge.
However, the conventional DNNs assume that testing data follows
the same distribution as that of the training data, which is
incongruent with the dynamic nature of real-world wireless
environments. This paper introduces a new training algorithm
designed speciﬁcally for dynamic wireless environments where
channel distribution exhibits variability. This method helps DNNs
adapt to new environments while preserving previously learned
information. The proposed approach distinguishes itself by
updating the DNN parameters in the null space of the low-rank
covariance of previous data, which reduces memory needs and
boosts training efﬁciency. Additionally, to counter the problem of
DNNs hitting their model capacity during continuous adaptation,
a selective forgetting mechanism is proposed. This mechanism
allows DNNs to discard the unimportant knowledge over time,
freeing up model capacity for more effective adaptation. The
effectiveness of the algorithm is validated by integrating it with
graph neural networks and multilayer perceptrons for weighted
sum-rate maximization. Through a comprehensive evaluation
that includes synthetic and ray-tracing-based datasets, superior
performance is demonstrated compared to existing methods.

I. I NTRODUCTION
D
EEP learning has demonstrated remarkable success in
wireless communications, including tasks such as power
allocation [2], [3], [4], [5], beamforming design [6], [7], [8],
and interference mitigation [9], [10], [11]. In these works, they
leverage deep neural networks (DNNs) to establish mapping
functions, thereby facilitating the acquisition of complicated
relationships between input information and their respective
solutions. Once adequately trained, these DNNs can rapidly
deduce solutions through real-time feed-forward computations.
Despite the impressive performance achieved by the DNN-
based methods, most of the existing works [2], [3], [4], [6],
[7], [8], [9], [10], [11] share a critical limitation: the impact
of the channel nonstationarity is not considered. Speciﬁcally,
these works assume that the data encountered follows the
same statistical distribution as that of the training dataset.
While the channel statistic remains stationary in a short time,
we aim to enable the adaptation of the DNN over a much
larger timescale, where the channel statistics evolve due to the
inherent channel nonstationary characteristic [12]. In particu-
lar, the channel distribution is parameterized by the positions
of scatterer clusters and the users [13]. As time progresses,
users’ positions, surrounding obstacles, and interference levels
change continuously, causing the parameters of the channel
distribution to evolve, leading to a continuously changing
channel distribution. The nonstationarity of the channels has
been validated through measurements in both static [14], [15],
[16] and mobile users’ equipment [17], [18]. Consequently,
a DNN trained on static or past data may fail to adapt to
these changes, leading to signiﬁcant performance degradation
when deployed in practical scenarios. It is crucial to investigate
a dynamic learning approach, where the DNN can adapt to
changes in the environment by learning from new datasets
with potentially ever-changing statistical distributions.
The dynamic learning paradigm can be represented in Fig. 1.
Every time the channel distribution changes signiﬁcantly, it
marks the transition to a new phase. It should be noticed that
although channel variations may be gradual, the cumulative
effect of these small changes can result in substantial shifts in
the channel distribution over time. For example, measurements
show that channel distribution changes signiﬁcantly every
3-4 hours in factories [15, Fig. 1], while in urban areas,
about 80% of cases have intervals longer than 60 minutes

Existing works on learning-based wireless resource alloca-
tion in dynamic environments can be categorized into two
major approaches: transfer/meta-learning [19], [20], [21] and
memory-based learning [22], [23], [24], [25].
A. Transfer/Meta-Learning
These methods involve training a DNN on an initial dataset
and then adjusting the parameters of the trained DNN using
new data to adapt to a changed environment. Both transfer
learning and meta-learning are able to improve the DNN’s
generalization ability on new data because they leverage the
shared knowledge between the original training dataset and
the new data, which enables quick adaptation to a dynamic
wireless environment. The primary difference between them
is that meta-learning achieves adaptation with fewer new data,
making it suitable for rapid DNN adaption.
B. Memory-Based Learning
This approach involves a memory set that is periodically
updated by combining a part of the most representative his-
torical samples and the new data from the changing wireless
environment [22], [23], [24]. Periodic updates to the memory
set and subsequent retraining of the DNN facilitate its adapta-
tion to the dynamic environment without forgetting previously
learned data.
However, simply exposing DNNs to continuously changing
real-world data, such as in transfer learning and meta-learning
methods [19], [20], [21], is not a fully feasible solution
due to a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting ,
which describes the tendency of DNNs to overwrite or dis-
card knowledge learned from their original training dataset
when exposed to new data from a dynamic environment
[26], [27]. Catastrophic forgetting has been widely observed
in deep learning-based wireless communications, including
system-level and link-level performance analysis [22], [28],
and channel estimation task [24]. When DNNs suffer from
catastrophic forgetting, they become adapted to the transient
environment, and performance on the previous environment
can degrade signiﬁcantly [22], [29]. Such instability is prob-
lematic since new data collected from the environment is often
much smaller in volume compared to the original training
dataset, increasing the risk of model overﬁtting [28]. Addition-
ally, channel nonstationarity stems from the random evolution
of clusters of scatterers and user mobility [13]. Since mobile
users periodically return to speciﬁc locations [30] and clusters
disappear and reappear over time [31], [32], [33], the data
learned previously will recur in the future.
Although memory-based learning can alleviate catastrophic
forgetting by storing past samples for retraining [22], this is
still a partial solution since the performance will be highly
degraded, especially for a large number of phases [26], [34],
[35]. This is because as the amount of data increases, the
number of samples from each phase retained in the memory
set inevitably decreases. This increases the likelihood of catas-
trophic forgetting, leading to poorer performance over time.
Furthermore, incorporating historical data in the retraining
cycles leads to considerable sample redundancy for each
retraining phase, which reduces the training efﬁciency [26].
Moreover, memorizing historical samples is not cost-effective,
especially when the memory resource is limited [36].
Recent advancements in machine learning might offer some
insights to address the above challenges. A pioneering work
[37] demonstrates that by projecting updates of trainable
parameters into the null space of the feature covariance
matrix, catastrophic forgetting can be effectively prevented
without relying on a memory set. However, experiments
have shown that although the null space approach effectively
mitigates catastrophic forgetting, it inadvertently limits the
DNN’s adaptability to new environments, especially when
the dimension of the null space is insufﬁcient [38], [39].
It is still a challenge to prevent catastrophic forgetting
while simultaneously maintaining satisfactory adaptability in
a training-efﬁcient and memory-efﬁcient manner.
This paper strives to overcome the above challenges by
proposing a novel learning method. Speciﬁcally, targeting
the enhancement of training and memory efﬁciency, we pro-
pose a dynamic learning framework that avoids catastrophic
forgetting while simultaneously adapting to changing environ-
ments in a dynamic learning setup, obviating the need for a
memory set. Within this framework, we ﬁrst establish a trade-
off between reducing catastrophic forgetting and increasing
adaptability, revealing that decreasing the rank of the fea-
ture covariance matrix can efﬁciently increase adaptability.
To improve adaptability without catastrophic forgetting, we
further propose a low-rank covariance approach that directly
constructs the feature covariance matrix in a low-rank man-
ner in each phase. Additionally, we introduce a selective
forgetting mechanism into the proposed low-rank covari-
ance approach to enhancing adaptability when the DNN’s

B. Proposed Dynamic Learning Framework
Given the dynamic nature of wireless environments, it is
imperative to design DNN-based methods that can adapt to
continuously evolving channel distributions. To maintain sat-
isfactory performance over time, the DNN should continually
learn from new data.
To accommodate these evolving distributions, we adopt the
episodic continual learning setup as shown in Fig. 1. The
passage of time in this environment is divided into T phases.
With each transition to a new phase, the distribution undergoes
a shift. Each phase is uniquely characterized by a descriptor
t ∈ {1, 2,..,T}, and is linked to a corresponding dataset
Dt ={Hi,t}nti=1 withnt denoting the number of samples. The
learning process is sequential: the DNN f(·;Wt−1), which
is trained up to phase t− 1, undergoes further training on
datasetDt in phase t to adapt to the new distribution. After
the completion of phase t, the updated DNN, i.e., f(·;Wt), is
expected to perform well on both Dt and all the previous data
fromD1 toDt−1.
However, this ambitious goal is challenging due to the
DNN’s vulnerability to catastrophic forgetting, which means
that the performance of the previous data can be severely
degraded. To prevent catastrophic forgetting, we propose the
following problem formulation for phase t:

III. D ILEMMA BETWEEN REDUCING CATASTROPHIC
FORGETTING AND INCREASING ADAPTABILITY
According to the deﬁnition of ¯Flt−1 before (4), we can
obtain the dimension of its null space as
Null(¯Flt−1) =al− rank(¯Flt−1), (8)
since ¯Flt−1 is a square matrix, and its rank signiﬁes its range
space dimension. As the training phase t increases, the rank
of ¯Flt−1 progressively increases due to the increasing column
number of the feature matrix ˜Flt−1 [1], [40]. According to (8),
a higher rank of ¯Flt−1 implies a lower dimension of its null
space. Therefore, if we maintain the stability condition (4),
the lower dimension of the null space of ¯Flt−1 will limit the
design space of ∆Wlt,s that satisﬁes the adaptability condition
(6) in Proposition 2.
On the other hand, to increase the adaptability, existing
approaches commonly approximate ¯Flt−1 by a low-rank matrix
so that the design space of ∆Wlt,s can be enlarged. Techniques
like SVD-based principal component selection [38] or rank-
constrained Frobenius norm minimization [39] can be adopted.
To enforce ∆Wlt,s only in the null space of the low-rank
approximation matrix instead of the original ¯Flt−1 as in (4),
the design space of ∆Wlt,s that satisﬁes (6) can be efﬁciently
enlarged. However, these approaches will destroy the original
stability condition (4), and thus increase catastrophic forget-
ting.
To better balance the trade-off between the stability and
the adaptability, we propose a low-rank covariance approach
to directly constructing ¯Flt−1 in a low-rank manner during
the phase training, such that the conventional two-step of
initial acquisition followed by a low-rank approximation can
be avoided. The proposed approach enlarges the design space
of ∆Wlt,s that satisﬁes (6) without violating (4), and hence
enhances the adaptability without compromising the stability,
as detailed in Section IV. Furthermore, to enhance the adapt-
ability when the DNN’s capacity is saturated, we additionally
introduce a selective forgetting mechanism into the proposed
low-rank covariance approach in Section V.

E. Training and Memory Efﬁciency Analyses
The proposed approach has two signiﬁcant advantages over
memory-based learning, which needs to store representative
data from previous phases in memory [22]. Firstly, compared
with memory-based learning with a memory size of nm,
which involvesnm +nt samples for each training phase t, the
proposed approach only uses nt training samples. Therefore,
the proposed approach is more training efﬁcient than the
memory-based learning approach.
Secondly, without the need for a memory set, the proposed
approach is also more memory efﬁcient. For example, for a
particular training phase t, if the memory size nm = 2000 ,
memory-based learning needs to store 2× 105 ﬂoating-point
numbers [22]. In contrast, the proposed approach only needs
to store the low-rank covariance matrix ¯Flt∈ Ral×al for l∈
{1, 2,...,L }. For a fair comparison, when the same MLP
as [22] is used with a1 = 100, a2 = 200, and a3 = 80, the
proposed approach only needs to store{¯Flt}3l=1 witha21+a22+
a23 = 5.64× 104 ﬂoating-point numbers. Additionally, given
that ¯Flt is the sum of Flt(Flt)T which comprises numerous
sparse elements as depicted in Fig. 2, ¯Flt inherently exhibits
sparsity, leading to further reduction in memory cost. It should
be noted that as the training phase t increases, memory-based
learning usually needs to continually increase the memory size
nm to avoid catastrophic forgetting [36]. In contrast, since the
size of the covariance matrix ¯Flt is independent of the number
of historical data, the memory cost will not increase as the
training phase t increases.
V. P ROPOSED SELECTIVE FORGETTING MECHANISM
As the phase number t increases, the rank of the matrix ¯Flt
inevitably grows due to the expanding number of columns in
the feature matrix ˜Flt [40]. Although our proposed low-rank
feature covariance can slow down the rate at which the rank
of ¯Flt increases (as shown in equation (8)), this growth cannot
be entirely halted. Over time, this leads to a reduced design
space for ∆Wlt,s, causing the DNN’s capacity to saturate and
its adaptability to diminish. Furthermore, saturation would also
occur if the DNN size is optimally selected during deployment.
In such cases, the DNN is designed with just enough capacity
to handle its initial training dataset, leaving no extra capacity
for adapting to new phases in a dynamic environment. To
address these limitations and enhance adaptability in both
scenarios, we propose a selective forgetting mechanism. This
mechanism selectively discards the least impactful information
in ¯Flt, thereby freeing up capacity within the DNN. By doing
so, we aim to maintain the adaptability of the DNN despite
the the number of training phases increases or initial capacity
constraints.
A. Measurement of DNN’s Saturation
To begin with, we need to determine when the DNN
has reached capacity saturation. To this end, we propose to
compare the absolute values of∆Wlt,s and Glt,s at each update

the projection into the null space of ¯Flt−1. If this residual
is comparable to the one before the projection, i.e., Glt,s, it
indicates that the projection itself does not affect the decrease
of the loss function. If the residual is much smaller than
the one before the projection, it means that the projection
hinders the decrease of the loss function, which results in
the DNN’s saturation with poor adaptability. To evaluate the
relative residue of ∆Wlt,s with respect to Glt,s, we adopt the

A. Benchmarks and Implementation Details
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed low-rank
covariance approach (LRCA), we compare it to the following
benchmarks:
• Transfer Learning (TL) [19]: This benchmark exclu-
sively updates the DNN with data from the current phase,
demonstrating how catastrophic forgetting can affect
DNN’s performance in changing wireless environments.
• Training with Historical Data (THD) : This approach
updates the DNN with all previously accumulated data
up to the current phase. However, it is important to note
that keeping data from all phases is impractical in real-
world scenarios. Therefore, this approach represents the
ideal performance, which serves as a performance upper
bound.
• Memory-Based Learning (MBL) [22]: This benchmark
involves a memory set that stores data from the current
phase and a selection of the most representative historical
samples. In order to identify the most representative
historical samples, bilevel optimization is used, which
prioritizes samples that contribute more signiﬁcantly to
the reduction of the DNN training loss.
• Null Space Method (NSCL) [37]: This approach updates
the DNN within the null space of the covariance matrix.
This benchmark aims to contrast the proposed low-rank
covariance approach on the overall performance.
We demonstrate the performance of the proposed approach
on two popular DNN architectures for wireless resource
allocation: MLP and GNN. The proposed approach based
on the two DNN architectures are correspondingly referred
to as LRCA-MLP and LRCA-GNN, respectively. The MLPs
in LRCA-MLP, TL, THD, and MBL comprise two hidden
layers, with 200 and 80 neurons, respectively [22]. For GNN,
we adopt the same architecture as in [6]. For all MLP and
GNN models, batch normalization is applied. Furthermore, we
consider both options of supervised and unsupervised learning.
For supervised learning, labels are generated by solving (1)
using the WMMSE algorithm [44]. On the other hand, in
unsupervised learning, the negative WSR is used for 𝓁(·,·).
Correspondingly, the two learning approaches are abbrevi-
ated as LRCA-GNN(MLP)-SL and LRCA-GNN(MLP)-UL,
respectively. It is worth noting that TL, THD, and MBL adopt
unsupervised learning.

VII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, a novel training algorithm for wireless
resource allocation has been proposed, facilitating adaptation
to ever-changing environments while retaining the perfor-
mance of previous data. The need for storing data from
previous environments is obviated by the proposed framework,
which enhances memory efﬁciency and improves training
efﬁciency. Moreover, the incorporation of low-rank feature
covariance within the framework ampliﬁes the DNNs’ adapt-
ability, making it suitable for long-term adaptation to changing
environments. Additionally, a selective forgetting mechanism
has been introduced to address the challenges of diminishing
DNN adaptability resulting from continuous environmental
shifts. This mechanism allows DNNs to selectively discard
the least impactful information, thereby bolstering their ability
to excel in dynamic environments. An extensive evalua-
tion utilizing both synthetic and ray-tracing-based datasets
has demonstrated the superior performance of the proposed
approach compared to existing methods.
